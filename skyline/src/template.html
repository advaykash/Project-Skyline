      What this number means

      It is: how many tracked satellites from known internet constellations are above the elevation limit at this place and time. Positions come from SGP4 using public orbital elements, the observer sits on the WGS-84 ellipsoid, and Earth's curvature is part of the geometry.

      It is not: proof that a satellite is working, that it serves this region, that a terminal is connected to it, or that service is available or licensed here. Ground stations, beams, capacity and regulation all decide that, and none of them are in the data.

      Historic breakups

      Three real events behind the debris in this app, adapted from akulbajpai-code/SkyLine (MIT). The facts come from the linked sources. The globe shows where the surviving catalogued fragments are now, propagated from current elements. It does not replay the events.

      11 January 2007 · anti-satellite test · about 865 km

      Fengyun-1C interception

      A retired Chinese weather satellite, Fengyun-1C (NORAD 25730), was struck by a ground-launched interceptor. The impact shattered the roughly 750 kg spacecraft into thousands of trackable fragments: the largest single debris cloud in low Earth orbit. At 865 km drag is weak, so most fragments will stay in orbit for decades.

      Sources: NASA Orbital Debris Program Office, CelesTrak

      10 February 2009 · accidental collision · about 776 km

      Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251

      The active communications satellite Iridium 33 (NORAD 24946) collided with the retired Russian satellite Cosmos 2251 (NORAD 22675) over Siberia at roughly 11.6 km/s. It was the first accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact satellites, and both were destroyed. Operators still track and dodge the two debris clouds.

      Sources: NASA ODPO, ESA Space Safety

      15 November 2021 · anti-satellite test · about 480 km

      Cosmos 1408 interception

      The retired Soviet satellite Cosmos 1408 (NORAD 13552) was struck by a ground-launched interceptor. Its debris cloud led to precautionary sheltering aboard the International Space Station. CelesTrak no longer publishes a separate group for it, so no fragments are plotted here and none are implied. At lower altitude, drag clears them faster than the 2007 cloud.

      Sources: NASA ODPO, ESA Space Safety

      Collision risk

      Not supported here. Judging collision risk needs validated conjunction data with position uncertainty, such as CelesTrak SOCRATES or Space-Track conjunction messages. Project Skyline does not load that data, so it never shows a collision probability. A close approach is two objects passing near each other. A collision means they actually hit.

      Why debris is a problem

      In low orbit everything moves at about 7.5 km/s, so even a 1 cm fragment hits with the energy of a hand grenade. Every collision makes more fragments, which raises the chance of the next one.

      How orbital tracking works

      Radar and telescopes on the ground, mainly the US Space Surveillance Network, watch objects as they pass overhead. Analysts fit an orbit to those observations and publish it as a short set of numbers called orbital elements (a TLE or OMM). CelesTrak redistributes these elements for free. To find where an object is at any moment, Project Skyline feeds its elements into SGP4, the standard model made for this data. SGP4 accounts for Earth's bulge and air drag.

      How SignalScope works

      SignalScope picks out satellites from known internet constellations by their catalog names (for example STARLINK, ONEWEB, KUIPER), and only if they are on CelesTrak's active list. Debris, rocket bodies and anything with an unrecognised name are never counted. For the chosen place it computes each satellite's elevation above the local horizon: the observer is placed on the WGS-84 ellipsoid, the satellite's SGP4 position is rotated into Earth-fixed coordinates, and the angle is measured against the local vertical, so Earth's curvature is included. The timeline samples the next hours at a fixed step; the count at the shown time is calculated from the current positions. Being above the horizon does not mean a satellite is working, serving that region, or connected to anyone.

      Orbital POV and pass replay

      Orbital POV puts the camera where an object is calculated to be: its SGP4 position from the latest CelesTrak elements, recomputed every frame at the time shown in the clock (simulator fragments use a simple two-body orbit). The view is drawn in the same space-fixed frame as everything else, with Earth turning under it. Horizon looks along the direction of travel, tilted down to the horizon, which sits about 20° below level from 420 km up. Nadir looks straight down with the direction of travel at the top. Free look lets you turn the view. It is a picture computed from orbital elements and texture maps, not imagery from the spacecraft: the Earth is a cloud-free composite and the city lights are fixed. The older the elements, the further the real object may be from this position (see Element age in the object details).

      Pass replay (Missions, Passes) is the opposite view: it looks up from your location while an object crosses your sky. Rise, peak and set times are found by searching the SGP4 positions down to half a second. Both use the same clock and the same orbit calculations.

      Why small debris is dangerous

      Speed, not size, makes debris deadly. Two objects in low orbit typically meet at about 10 km/s, which is ten times faster than a rifle bullet. Kinetic energy is ½ × mass × speed², so a 1 cm aluminium ball weighing 1.4 g carries about 70 kJ at 10 km/s. That is roughly the energy of 17 g of TNT. Objects smaller than about 10 cm usually are not tracked at all, and ESA estimates there are around a million fragments between 1 and 10 cm.

      The Kessler cascade

      In 1978 NASA scientist Donald Kessler warned that once orbits get crowded enough, collisions create debris faster than air drag removes it, and each collision makes the next more likely. Two events in this data show how fast clouds form. In 2007 an anti-satellite missile test destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite. In 2009 the working Iridium 33 and the dead Cosmos 2251 collided by accident, the first collision between two intact satellites.

      When orbits break apart

      Three events made most of the catalogued debris in this data: the 2007 Fengyun-1C anti-satellite test, the 2009 collision of Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251, and the 2021 Cosmos 1408 test. Simulations has a card for each, with a button that shows the surviving fragments on the globe.

      Why this matters

      Communications: satellite internet, TV and phone links for ships, planes and remote areas. Navigation: GPS gives position and precise time to phones, banks and power grids. Weather: polar and geostationary weather satellites feed every forecast and storm warning. Climate and Earth observation: crops, wildfires, ice and sea level are monitored from orbit. Exploration: astronauts on the ISS have had to move the station out of the way of debris many times. If key orbits fill with fragments, all of these get riskier and more expensive.

      Limits and uncertainty

      Positions from public elements are approximate: typically around a kilometre off when the elements are fresh, and drifting by kilometres per day as they age. Each object panel shows its element age. Solar activity changes air drag, which is the biggest source of error in low orbit. Some objects are not in public data, and most debris is too small to track. Project Skyline loads only some CelesTrak groups, so its counts are a subset of the catalog. Optional clouds are decorative, and dots are not to scale.
